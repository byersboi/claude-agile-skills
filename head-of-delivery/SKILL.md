# Head of Delivery

The Head of Delivery Management owns the **profession**, not the programme. Where an Agile Delivery Manager is accountable for delivering value through one or more teams, the Head of Delivery is accountable for the organisation having the delivery capability it needs — the right people, at the right level, deployed to the right work, growing over time.

The UK Government Digital and Data (DDaT) capability framework describes the role as "an experienced practitioner who exemplifies what good looks like across the delivery roles". That phrase carries the whole job: you must still be credible as a practitioner, and you are now judged on other people's practice rather than your own.

**The boundary that matters** — if you find yourself running a programme's RAID log, chairing its dependency board, or writing its stakeholder report, you have quietly taken an ADM job. See the `agile-delivery-manager` skill for that work. Your job is that the ADM doing it is good enough that you don't have to.

## What the role actually covers

From the DDaT role description, the accountabilities are:

- **Championing the role** within and across the organisation — defending what delivery managers are for when people try to turn them into project administrators
- **Leading the community of practice** and building capability
- **Recruiting** into delivery roles
- **Supporting professional development** — assessment, progression, coaching, career paths
- **Promoting cross-functional collaboration** between delivery, product, user-centred design, technology and operations
- **Communicating value to stakeholders** across technical and non-technical audiences
- **Maintaining credibility and influence** across departments

Four of those seven are about people and profession. Plan your diary accordingly — most Heads of Delivery under-invest here because delivery firefighting is louder and more immediately rewarding.

## The capability framework

DDaT defines **nine skills** for the delivery management job family, assessed at four levels: awareness, working, practitioner, expert. For the Head of Delivery role, six skills are classed essential and three desirable — but note that most of the "desirable" ones are still pitched at expert.

| Skill | Head of Delivery requires | Essential / Desirable* |
|---|---|---|
| Agile and Lean practices | Expert | Essential |
| Communicating between the technical and non-technical | Expert | Essential |
| Maintaining delivery momentum | Expert | Essential |
| Making a process work | Expert | Essential |
| Planning | Expert | Essential |
| Team dynamics and collaboration | Expert | Essential |
| Commercial management | Expert | Desirable |
| Life cycle management | Expert | Desirable |
| Financial management | Practitioner | Desirable |

\*The essential/desirable split comes from the GOV.UK role publication; the framework site itself publishes only the required levels. Treat the levels as the harder fact.

This is a useful spine even outside government: it is externally validated, written in outcome language rather than certification language, and it gives you a shared vocabulary for assessment, hiring and progression. Adopt the skill names wholesale; adapt the wording to your context.

## The job family: what progression actually looks like

The real value of the framework to a Head of Delivery is the whole ladder, not just your own row. This is your career path conversation, your job spec source, and your assessment frame:

| Skill | Associate DM | Delivery Manager | Senior DM | Head of Delivery |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Agile and Lean practices | Working | Practitioner | Expert | Expert |
| Commercial management | Working | Working | Practitioner | Expert |
| Communicating technical / non-technical | Working | Practitioner | Expert | Expert |
| Financial management | Awareness | Working | Practitioner | Practitioner |
| Life cycle management | Awareness | Working | Practitioner | Expert |
| Maintaining delivery momentum | Working | Practitioner | Expert | Expert |
| **Making a process work** | Working | **Expert** | Expert | Expert |
| Planning | Awareness | Practitioner | Expert | Expert |
| Team dynamics and collaboration | Awareness | Working | Practitioner | Expert |

**Read the anomalies — they're the point:**

- **"Making a process work" is expert from Delivery Manager upwards.** The framework is saying that challenging disproportionate organisational process is not a senior privilege; it is the core of the job from day one of being a DM. Most organisations get this exactly backwards and expect junior DMs to comply.
- **Team dynamics and life cycle management climb every rung** — awareness → working → practitioner → expert, one step per role. They are the slowest-building skills in the family. Team dynamics also carries by far the longest expert descriptor in the framework, which tells you where the real difficulty at Head level sits.
- **Financial management tops out at practitioner.** You are not expected to be an accountant. You are expected to negotiate and set budgets in complex environments and write business cases.
- **Only three skills change between Senior DM and Head**: commercial management, life cycle management, and team dynamics and collaboration, each practitioner → expert. The other six are identical. Stepping up is not broad uplift — it is three specific skills, and two of them (commercial, life cycle) are the ones senior DMs have usually had least exposure to. That is your promotion conversation.

## The six essential skills in practice

Each section below gives the DDaT expert descriptor, then the practitioner's read: what it looks like present, what it looks like absent, and how to probe for it.

### 1. Agile and Lean practices

**Expert:** coach and lead teams in agile and lean good practices; create and tailor the right approach for a team, challenging, evaluating and iterating through the life cycle; experiment with new and innovative ways of working to improve delivery across the organisation; act as a recognised expert and advocate.

**Present:** they explain *why* a practice exists and will happily drop it when it stops serving the outcome. They talk about feedback loops and flow, not ceremonies and artefacts. They bring ideas in from outside.

**Absent:** framework fundamentalism ("that's not real Scrum"), or its mirror image — "we're agile, we don't plan". Both are the same failure: practice as identity rather than tool.

**Probe:** "Tell me about a time you removed or changed an agile practice. What made you do it, and what happened?" Weak answers describe adding practices. Strong answers describe subtraction, with evidence.

### 2. Communicating between the technical and non-technical

**Expert:** mediate between people and strengthen relationships, adopting the appropriate communication method with stakeholders at all levels; manage expectations and moderate difficult discussions about high-risk and complex topics, even within constrained timescales; speak on behalf of and represent the community to large audiences inside and outside the organisation.

**Present:** the same message survives translation up and down. Bad news arrives early, with options attached. Engineers trust them not to misrepresent technical reality to leadership.

**Absent:** the "translation layer" that softens risk on the way up and hardens commitments on the way down. Teams discover what was promised on their behalf after the fact.

**Probe:** "Describe the hardest conversation you've had with a senior stakeholder. What did you say in the first two minutes?"

### 3. Maintaining delivery momentum

**Expert:** optimise the delivery flow of teams; actively address the most complicated risks, issues and dependencies including where ownership exists outside the team or where no clear ownership exists; identify innovative ways to unblock issues.

**Present:** they go after unowned problems. They work on the *system* producing the blockages — the approval that always takes three weeks — not just today's blockage.

**Absent:** impediment logs that are really escalation lists. Long-lived blockers with a named owner who was never consulted.

**Probe:** "What's the longest-running blocker you've dealt with, and how did you finally shift it?" Listen for whether they changed the system or just applied pressure.

### 4. Making a process work

**Expert:** identify and challenge organisational processes of increasing complexity and those that are unnecessarily complicated; add value and coach the organisation to inspect and adapt its processes; guide teams through implementing a new process.

**Present:** they can name three organisational processes they have changed, and describe how they built the case with evidence rather than complaint.

**Absent:** compliance as a shrug — "that's just how governance works here". Or the opposite: bypassing governance and burning credibility for a short-term win.

**Probe:** "Which process in your last organisation cost the most and delivered the least? What did you do about it?"

This is the most under-developed skill in most delivery populations, because it requires organisational courage rather than delivery technique. It is also where you add the most leverage — a DM can challenge a process for one programme; you can change it for everyone.

### 5. Planning

**Expert:** lead a continual planning process in a very complex environment; plan beyond product delivery; identify dependencies in plans across services and co-ordinate delivery; coach other teams as the central point of expertise.

**Present:** probabilistic language — ranges and confidence levels rather than single dates. Plans visibly revised as evidence arrives, with assumptions written down.

**Absent:** the annual plan nobody has reopened since January, and the "we'll know when we get there" refusal to forecast at all.

**Probe:** "How do you answer a director who asks when something will be done, when you genuinely don't know?" See `monte-carlo-forecasting` and `agile-metrics` for the mechanics.

### 6. Team dynamics and collaboration

**Expert:** change organisational structures to fixable and sustainable designs; lead on strategy for an entire organisation, joining up business needs with innovative analysis; make and justify decisions characterised by high levels of risk, impact and complexity; build consensus between organisations or highly independent and diverse stakeholders; solve and unblock issues within teams or departments at the highest level; understand the psychology of a team and have strong mediation skills; coach an organisation on team dynamics and conflict resolution.

**Present:** they will reshape a team or a reporting line to fix a delivery problem, and can explain the trade-off they accepted. They intervene in conflict early and in person.

**Absent:** team composition treated as fixed; conflict routed to HR or left to burn; "personality clash" used as a diagnosis rather than a starting point.

**Probe:** "Tell me about a team you restructured. Who disagreed, and how did you handle it?" See `conflict-management` and `team-health`.

## The three desirable skills

**Commercial management (expert)** — act as the escalation point and resolve large or high-risk commercial issues; coach others in commercial management. In practice: contract shape, supplier performance, and recognising when a commercial model is working against agile delivery. One of the three skills that steps up from senior DM — see `supplier-management`.

**Life cycle management (expert)** — lead and coach teams through different phases of the life cycle; ensure effective support for the product or service and its users; predict, prevent, mitigate and manage potential risks at different phases; contribute to the assessment of other teams, providing guidance and support. The commonest gap is the back end: plenty of delivery managers have never run a live service or retired anything. See `release-management`.

**Financial management (practitioner)** — negotiate, influence or set budgets in complex environments; write or input into business cases and communicate business-value propositions. Argue for persistent team funding over project funding while you're there. See `lean-portfolio-management`.

## Assessing your delivery population

Use the nine skills and four levels as the assessment frame; the published descriptors give you the wording for each cell.

**How to run it:**

1. **Self-assessment first.** The delivery manager scores themselves across all nine skills before you score anything. This is a development conversation, not a performance review — say so explicitly and mean it.
2. **Evidence, not opinion.** Every claimed level needs a specific example. "Practitioner in planning" is meaningless; "led replanning across four teams when the integration date moved" is assessable.
3. **Triangulate.** Your view, their view, and what you have seen directly by observing them in a planning session, a stakeholder meeting, or a difficult conversation. Observation beats both opinions.
4. **Compare against the role, not the ceiling.** A DM at working level on team dynamics is exactly where the framework expects them to be. Assess the gap to their current role first, then the gap to the next one.
5. **Two development areas, not nine.** Agree the smallest number of skills that would make the biggest difference, and a concrete way to practise each.
6. **Aggregate, anonymised, into a capability heatmap.** Nine skills × your population tells you where the systemic gaps are — a hiring, training and deployment input, not a league table. Never publish individual scores.

**Anti-pattern:** turning the framework into a promotion gate. The moment levels determine pay, self-assessment becomes negotiation and the honest signal disappears.

## Building capability

**Community of practice.** Your primary instrument. Make it a working session, not a status meeting — real problems brought by real practitioners, with someone leaving the room with an action. Rotate facilitation so it isn't your show. If attendance is dropping, the content is too abstract. See `facilitation` for session design and `sm-mentoring` for the parallel Scrum Master community.

**Deployment as development.** The most powerful development lever you hold is who you put on what. A practitioner-level DM on a stretch programme with an expert peer nearby will develop faster than on any course. Track deployment decisions against development needs, not just against demand.

**Pairing and shadowing.** Pair a DM with a commercial management gap with one who has it, on a live procurement. Cheaper and stickier than training — and commercial is the hardest gap to close from a classroom.

**Your own practice.** Credibility decays. Stay close enough to real delivery — facilitate something occasionally, sit in on planning — that you are not coaching from memory. "Exemplifies what good looks like" is a requirement, not a compliment.

## Hiring delivery managers

**Structure the interview around the skills, not the CV.** Pick four or five of the nine relevant to the level you're hiring, and probe each with behavioural questions. Same questions for every candidate; score against the level descriptors.

**What to weight heavily:**
- *Making a process work* and *maintaining delivery momentum* — these separate a delivery manager from a delivery administrator, and they are hard to teach. The framework expects expert-level process challenge from DM level upwards; test it properly rather than treating it as a senior-only skill
- Evidence of having changed something organisational, not just run something well
- How they talk about teams they have worked with — generosity or blame tells you most of what you need

**What to weight lightly:**
- Certifications. They evidence attendance, not capability
- Framework fluency. Easily learned; frequently mistaken for the job
- Sector experience, unless the domain is genuinely specialist

**Red flags:** describes delivery entirely in terms of ceremonies run and reports produced; cannot name a failure; refers to "my teams" possessively rather than accountably; blames stakeholders for everything that went wrong.

**Include a practitioner on the panel.** A DM interviewed only by managers tells you how they perform upwards, which is half the job at most.

## Anti-patterns for the role

| Anti-pattern | Why it happens | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| **The super-ADM** | Delivery pain is visible and urgent; capability building isn't | Protect diary time for the profession; coach the ADM instead of covering for them |
| **The reporting layer** | Leadership asks you for status, repeatedly | Push reporting to the teams that own the work; give leadership one honest aggregate view |
| **Framework policeman** | Standardisation feels like leadership | Standardise outcomes and the interfaces between teams; let teams choose practice |
| **Hiring in your own image** | Comfort and speed | Assess against the framework; deliberately hire the skills your heatmap says you lack |
| **Capability theatre** | Training budget is easier to spend than deployment is to change | Measure capability by what people can now do unsupported, not courses attended |
| **Levels as pay bands** | HR asks for objective criteria | Keep assessment developmental; use separate evidence for promotion decisions |
| **Absent from the work** | Diary fills with leadership forums | Spend a session a week with a team; credibility is maintained, not earned once |

## What to do first, if you're new to the role

1. **Meet every delivery manager individually** before changing anything. Ask what's in their way. Data collection and relationship building at once — see `sm-one-to-ones` for structure.
2. **Sit in on delivery** — a planning session, a review, a stakeholder meeting. Observe, don't intervene.
3. **Build the capability heatmap** from self-assessment plus your observations. You now know your gaps and your bench.
4. **Find the one organisational process** costing the most across all teams, and go after it. An early systemic win establishes you as something other than another layer of management.
5. **Restart or reshape the community of practice** — it is the engine for everything else.
6. **Agree with leadership what you are accountable for**, in writing. If nobody has drawn the line between you and the programmes, it will be drawn for you, badly.

## Capability health metrics

Measure the profession, not the programmes:

- **Capability coverage** — % of skills at the level their role requires, across the population; movement over time matters more than the absolute number
- **Bench depth** — how many people could step up a level if a key role opened tomorrow
- **Time to productive** — how long a new delivery manager takes to run a team unsupported
- **Retention and progression** — internal moves up the job family; regretted leavers
- **Community engagement** — attendance is weak signal; contributions and actions completed are strong
- **Systemic impediments removed** — organisational blockers fixed for everyone, not once per programme

Avoid aggregating team delivery metrics (velocity, throughput) upward as a measure of *your* performance. It creates exactly the pressure that breaks those metrics — see `agile-metrics`.
