# Remote Facilitation Tool Selection

Choosing and using tooling for virtual workshops. For ceremony-specific setup — planning, retro, review — see `agile-ceremonies/references/facilitation-tools.md`. For technique, see the `facilitation` skill.

## Choose tools last

Session design first: purpose, outcomes, decisions, agenda. Only then ask what tooling the agenda needs. A workshop designed around a tool's features is a demo, not a session.

**Three selection rules:**
1. **Fewest tools that do the job.** Each additional tool costs five minutes of setup and excludes someone
2. **What participants already have.** Adoption beats capability
3. **Access verified beforehand.** Suppliers, contractors and people from other departments are the ones locked out, and you'll find out at 09:02

---

## By job

| Job | Options | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Shared visual space** | Miro, Mural, FigJam, Excalidraw | Miro/Mural for large structured workshops; FigJam lighter and faster to learn; Excalidraw free and minimal |
| **Video and breakouts** | Teams, Zoom, Meet | Check breakout room capability and whether external guests can join them — the commonest nasty surprise |
| **Polls and voting** | Built-in polls, Slido, Mentimeter | Built-in is usually enough; specialist tools for large groups or anonymous ranking |
| **Async input** | Shared docs, forms, wiki | For pre-work, pre-reads and gathering input before the session |
| **Anonymous input** | Retro tools, forms, Mentimeter | Necessary when safety is low; whiteboards are not anonymous however much people believe they are |
| **Timers** | Built into whiteboards, or a shared screen timer | Visible to everyone, not just you |
| **Documentation** | Whatever the team already uses | Outputs must land where people work, not in the whiteboard |

**On anonymity:** if you promise it, make sure the tool actually delivers it. Most whiteboards record authorship in some form. Teams find out, and you only get to lose that trust once.

## Setting up a virtual workshop

**A week before**
- Build the board: one clearly labelled area per activity, in session order
- Add the agenda and the purpose to the board itself — people arrive without reading the invite
- Pre-configure breakout rooms with the groups you want
- Send pre-work with a deadline that isn't the morning of

**The day before**
- Test the board with someone who has the same access level as your least-privileged participant
- Prepare a fallback — if the whiteboard is down, can you run this in a document? (Usually yes.)

**Fifteen minutes before**
- Open the room early; be there
- Board locked to prevent accidental dragging of other people's content, unlocked when you start

**Opening (first 5 minutes)**
- Purpose and outcomes, stated plainly
- How the session will run, and what's expected of people
- A two-minute board orientation — one round of "everyone put a dot next to your name" gets hands working before it matters
- Working agreements: cameras, chat, interruptions, breaks

## Facilitating in a virtual room

**Structural participation beats calling on people.** Write-first activities, polls, breakouts and explicit rounds. "Any thoughts?" produces silence in every remote room ever convened.

**Get a co-facilitator.** One person cannot run the content, watch chat, track hands, manage time and notice who hasn't spoken. Split it — content and process.

**Breakout rooms:**
- Three to four people; five starts to need its own facilitator
- Written instructions posted in the room and on the board — people forget verbal instructions within thirty seconds of the room switching
- Assign each group a named area on the board before they go
- Announce the time remaining; pop in once, briefly
- Report back into a shared structure, not a round of five-minute presentations

**Timing:** 90 minutes maximum without a real break. Four-hour workshops become two sessions on different days. Attention on video is a hard constraint, not a preference.

**Silence:** count to ten before filling it. It always feels longer remotely.

## Hybrid

Assume it will default to two meetings — the room and the observers — and design against that.

- **Everyone on their own device**, including people in the room. No shared laptop at the end of the table
- **Digital-first artefacts.** A physical wall excludes half the participants; move it to the board
- **Remote speaks first** on every round
- **A remote advocate** in the room whose only job is watching for remote hands and pulling them in
- **Audio is the thing to spend money on.** A good room microphone matters more than the camera; remote participants drop out of conversations they can't hear

## Accessibility

- Don't rely on colour alone to convey meaning
- Check contrast on board templates — the default pastel sticky notes often fail
- Turn on live captions where available
- Share the board and any slides in advance for those who want to read ahead
- Allow camera-off without justification
- Build in breaks and don't run over — nobody's home circumstances are your business

## Anti-patterns

| Anti-pattern | Why it hurts | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Learning the tool during the session | Twenty minutes of the workshop lost | Two-minute orientation activity at the start; simpler tool if needed |
| Board built live | Chaotic; participants watch you type | Build it in advance |
| One tool per activity | Login friction, exclusion, lost context | Consolidate to one board |
| No co-facilitator | Chat unread, hands missed, time overruns | Split content and process |
| Four-hour video workshop | Nothing after hour two is usable | Split across days; break every 90 minutes |
| Outputs left on the board | Never seen again | Move decisions and actions into the team's working tools before the session ends |
| Promised anonymity the tool doesn't give | Trust lost permanently | Verify first, or don't promise it |
| Hybrid via room camera alone | Remote participants become spectators | Everyone on their own device; remote first |
