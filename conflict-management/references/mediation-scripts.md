# Mediation Scripts

Conversation guides for common conflict scenarios. Use them as structure, not as a script to read — the words should be yours. For diagnosing which situation you're in first, see `references/conflict-assessment-tools.md`.

## Before any mediated conversation

**Check you're the right person.** You need neutrality, and both parties need to believe you have it. If you line-manage one of them, or have a stake in the outcome, find someone else.

**Meet each person separately first.** Never open with a joint session. Separate conversations tell you whether joint mediation is viable at all, and prevent the first joint session from being the first time either has said any of it out loud.

**Contract explicitly at the start of the joint session:**
> "I'm here to help the two of you have a conversation, not to decide who's right. What's said here stays between us unless we agree otherwise. I'll interrupt if we get personal or if one of us is doing all the talking. Are you both willing to work on this?"

A "no" to that last question is important information. Don't push past it.

## 1. Pre-mediation individual conversation

Same structure with each party, same amount of time, ideally the same day.

> **Open:** "I want to understand how this looks from where you're sitting. Take me through it."
>
> **Then:**
> - "What's the impact of this on you? On your work?"
> - "What do you think it looks like from their side?"
> - "What have you already tried?"
> - "What would a good outcome look like — realistically?"
> - "What would you be willing to change?"
> - "Is there anything you wouldn't want said in a joint conversation?"
>
> **Close:** "I'm having the same conversation with them. If you're both willing, I'd like to bring you together. Here's how that would work…"

**Listen for:** whether they can articulate the other's position at all (if not, you're at Glasl stage 4+), and whether "what I'd be willing to change" produces anything. A party with nothing to offer isn't ready for a joint session.

## 2. Two team members in open conflict

**Structure:**

1. **Frame (2 min).** "You've both told me this isn't working. The point of the next hour is for each of you to hear the other properly, and then to agree how you'll work together from here."
2. **Each speaks uninterrupted (5 min each).** "A first, then B. No interruptions — you'll each get the same time."
3. **Reflect back.** "B, tell me what you heard A say — not whether you agree." This is the move that changes the room, and the one people resist most. Hold it.
4. **Find the shared ground.** "You both want this release to go well. You disagree about how. Is that fair?"
5. **Move to behaviour.** "What specifically do you need the other person to do differently?" Concrete, observable, small.
6. **Agree.** Two or three commitments each, with a review date.
7. **Close.** "We'll check in in two weeks. If it slips, either of you tells me — early."

**If it heats up:**
> "Let's pause. We've moved from the issue to each other. I want to go back to the specific thing that happened on Tuesday."

**If one dominates:**
> "I'm going to stop you there — I want to make sure we hear the same amount from both of you."

## 3. Giving difficult feedback (SBI)

Private, close in time to the event, and about behaviour you observed.

> **Situation:** "In yesterday's planning session…"
> **Behaviour:** "…when you cut across [colleague] twice while she was explaining the risk…"
> **Impact:** "…she didn't speak again for the rest of the session, and we didn't hear the rest of her point. I'm concerned we lost something important."
> **Then stop.** Let them respond. This pause is the whole technique.
> **Forward:** "What was going on for you?" … "What would you do differently next time?"

**Do not:** stack three examples, open with praise as camouflage, or attribute intent ("you clearly don't respect her"). Name what you saw, name the effect, ask.

**If they push back hard:** "I might be reading it wrong — that's why I'm raising it rather than assuming. What did you see?"

## 4. Team member versus Product Owner over scope

Usually a process conflict wearing a relationship costume.

> **Open:** "You two are ending up on opposite sides every planning session. I'd like to work out why the process is putting you there."
>
> **To the developer:** "What's the concern behind pushing back on scope?"
> **To the PO:** "What's the pressure you're under that the team doesn't see?"
>
> **Then reframe:** "So you're worried about quality debt we'll pay for in three months, and you're worried about a commitment you've made to [stakeholder] that you can't easily move. Neither of those goes away by arguing in planning."
>
> **Move to the structural fix:** "What would have to change so this gets resolved before planning rather than during it?"

Common resolutions: refinement earlier, the PO bringing stakeholder pressure to the team as information rather than instruction, explicit capacity for quality work, or the SM taking the stakeholder conversation off the PO.

## 5. Someone being undermined in public

Address it twice: in the moment, lightly; afterwards, properly.

**In the moment:**
> "Hang on — [name] hadn't finished. Go on."

Short, neutral, no lecture. A public correction of the behaviour, not of the person.

**Afterwards, privately with the person who did it:** SBI, as above.

**Afterwards, privately with the person affected:**
> "I noticed what happened in there, and I've spoken to [name] about it. How are you doing? Is this a pattern or a one-off? What would help?"

Never leave it at the in-the-moment intervention alone. The affected person needs to know it was seen.

---

## 6. Team versus a person who isn't pulling their weight

The one people most often get wrong, by mediating between the team and the individual. Don't. That's a pile-on.

**With the team, first:** "What specifically isn't happening? Give me examples, not impressions." Separate performance concerns (their manager's job) from working-relationship concerns (yours).

**With the individual, privately:**
> "I want to check in on how things are going for you. From where I sit, [specific observation]. What's going on?"

Often the answer is something the team doesn't know — health, home, a skills gap they're embarrassed by, or work being routed around them until they've disengaged.

**Then decide:** if it's capability or conduct, hand it to their line manager with a clear description; it is not the Scrum Master's job and pretending otherwise fails everyone. If it's a working-relationship problem, facilitate — often it's the team that needs to change a behaviour, not only the individual.

---

## 7. Escalating when mediation has failed

To the line manager, in writing after the conversation:

> "I've facilitated two conversations between [A] and [B] over the past month. We agreed [specific commitments]; those haven't held, and the effect on the team is [concrete impact]. I don't think further facilitation from me will help — it's now affecting delivery and the wider team. I'd like to hand this to you. Here's what I've done so far and what I'd suggest."

**Tell both parties you are escalating, before you do it.** Escalation they discover is a betrayal; escalation they're told about is a consequence.

## Phrases worth having ready

| Situation | Phrase |
|---|---|
| Discussion goes personal | "Let's come back to what actually happened." |
| Someone speaks for the absent | "Let's find out rather than assume." |
| Circling with no progress | "We've been round this twice. What's the thing we're not saying?" |
| One party won't engage | "You seem reluctant. What would make this worth doing for you?" |
| Agreement feels too quick | "That was fast. What's the bit either of you is still unhappy about?" |
| You're losing neutrality | "I'm too close to this to be useful. Let me find someone who isn't." |
| Emotion runs high | "Let's take ten minutes. This matters enough to do properly." |
