# Conflict Assessment Tools

Diagnostics for working out what kind of conflict you're dealing with before you intervene. For the models themselves — Thomas-Kilmann modes, Glasl's escalation ladder, SBI — see the `conflict-management` skill. For the conversations, see `references/mediation-scripts.md`.

## Diagnose before you intervene

The commonest mistake is intervening in the conflict you can see rather than the one that's driving it. Three questions, in order:

1. **What kind of conflict is this?** Task, process, relationship or status
2. **How escalated is it?** Glasl's nine stages, grouped into three phases
3. **Whose is it to resolve?** Theirs, yours to facilitate, or someone else's to decide

---

## 1. Conflict type diagnostic

| Type | About | Healthy? | Signals |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Task** | What we should do — priorities, design, approach | Yes, in moderation — it improves decisions | Disagreement is specific, cites evidence, ends when a decision is made |
| **Process** | How we work — who decides, who does what, ways of working | Sometimes — surfaces unclear agreements | Recurring friction at the same point in the workflow; "that's not my job" |
| **Relationship** | Each other — trust, respect, personality | No — corrodes team performance | Personal language, eye-rolling, exclusion, complaints outside the room |
| **Status** | Position, influence, recognition | No — but common and rarely named | Credit disputes, undermining in public, resistance tied to who proposed something |

**The pattern worth knowing:** unresolved task conflict becomes relationship conflict within a few weeks. People who cannot resolve *what* start attributing motives to *who*. If a technical disagreement has become personal, resolve the technical decision first — it often dissolves the rest.

**Status conflict is the most frequently misdiagnosed.** If someone opposes every proposal from one colleague and none from anyone else, the disagreement is not about the proposals.

## 2. Escalation assessment (Glasl, condensed)

Score where the conflict currently sits. The stage determines who can help, which matters more than any other judgement you make here.

**Phase 1 — win/win (stages 1–3): the parties can still solve it themselves**
- 1 *Tension*: occasional friction, still cooperating
- 2 *Debate*: positions harden, arguments become tactical
- 3 *Actions over words*: people stop discussing and start acting unilaterally

→ **Your role: facilitate.** A structured conversation between the two of them, with you holding the process, is usually enough.

**Phase 2 — win/lose (stages 4–6): they need a third party**
- 4 *Coalitions*: recruiting supporters; the conflict acquires an audience
- 5 *Loss of face*: public discrediting; the relationship becomes the issue
- 6 *Threat strategies*: ultimatums, escalation threats, resignation talk

→ **Your role: mediate, or find someone who can.** Self-resolution is no longer realistic. At stage 6, involve management.

**Phase 3 — lose/lose (stages 7–9): intervention from above is required**
- 7 *Limited destruction*: damage to the other accepted as a cost worth paying
- 8 *Fragmentation*: attacking the other's position and support base
- 9 *Together into the abyss*: mutual destruction accepted

→ **Your role: escalate.** This is beyond facilitation and usually beyond mediation. HR and line management, quickly.

**The practitioner's rule:** most workplace conflict is stage 2–4, and most of it is left alone until it reaches 5. Intervening at 2 costs a twenty-minute conversation; intervening at 5 costs weeks.

## 3. Team conflict diagnostic

Run when the team as a whole feels off, and you can't name why. Score each *rarely / sometimes / often*.

**Healthy conflict**
- We disagree openly in meetings and it doesn't feel dangerous
- Decisions get properly debated before they're made
- We can challenge the most senior person in the room
- Disagreements end in a decision people commit to, even when they lost

**Warning signs**
- People agree in the room and complain afterwards
- The same disagreement resurfaces every few weeks without resolving
- Certain topics are never raised
- One or two people dominate every discussion
- Decisions get quietly reversed after the meeting

**Danger signs**
- Personal remarks, sarcasm or eye-rolling in public
- People route around a colleague rather than working with them
- Information is withheld between team members
- Someone has stopped contributing entirely
- Complaints are arriving through line managers or HR

**Reading it:** all of the first block and little else means the team is doing conflict well — leave it alone. Warning signs cluster around *avoided* conflict, which is the commonest dysfunction and the one that looks like harmony from the outside. Any danger sign means intervene this week, not at the next retro.

## 4. Personal style check (Thomas-Kilmann, quick version)

For a coaching conversation, not a psychometric. Ask someone to pick which statement fits how they *usually* behave under disagreement:

- "I push for my position; someone has to decide" → **Competing**
- "I look for what works for both of us, even if it takes longer" → **Collaborating**
- "I look for the middle ground quickly" → **Compromising**
- "I'd rather let it go than damage the relationship" → **Accommodating**
- "I wait and hope it resolves itself" → **Avoiding**

**The coaching value isn't the label — it's the range.** Every mode is right sometimes. The question worth asking: *"When did you last use a different mode from that one, and what happened?"* People with one mode are the ones who struggle; people with three or four adapt.

**Team-level:** if everyone answers accommodating or avoiding, the team's harmony is a symptom, not an achievement.

## 5. Is it conflict, or is it the system?

Before mediating anything between two people, check whether you would be treating a symptom:

- Are these two people set up to compete — for the same resource, credit, or role clarity they don't have?
- Does the process force them into a hand-off that nobody owns?
- Are they being given contradictory priorities by different people?
- Is one of them carrying accountability without the authority to act?
- Would a new pair of people in these roles have the same argument within a month?

If yes to any, the intervention is structural. Mediating the relationship will feel productive and change nothing.

## Deciding what to do

| Assessment | Action |
|---|---|
| Task conflict, stages 1–2 | Leave it. Healthy disagreement; protect the space for it |
| Task conflict recurring, no resolution | The decision rights are unclear — fix that, not the relationship |
| Process conflict | Working agreements session; make the implicit explicit |
| Relationship conflict, stages 1–3 | Facilitated conversation between the two |
| Relationship conflict, stages 4–6 | Formal mediation; consider involving management |
| Any conflict at stages 7–9 | Escalate to HR and line management immediately |
| Status conflict | Rarely resolvable inside the team — name it privately with each party, then involve their manager |
| Systemic cause identified | Change the structure; tell both parties that's what you're doing |
