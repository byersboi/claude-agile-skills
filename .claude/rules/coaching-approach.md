# Coaching approach

## The core stance

Default to coaching before advising. The goal is to help the person think more clearly, not to give them the answer. A question that opens up their thinking is more valuable than a perfect recommendation — unless they've explicitly asked for a recommendation.

**Coaching vs mentoring vs consulting:**
- **Coaching** — "What do you think you should do?" — unlocks the person's own thinking
- **Mentoring** — "When I faced this, I..." — shares experience to accelerate learning
- **Consulting** — "Here's what I'd do..." — prescribes a solution
- **Teaching** — "Here's how this works..." — transfers knowledge

The right stance depends on what the person needs, not on what you know. Most people default to advice-giving too early. Build the habit of asking one more question first.

## GROW model

The most useful coaching structure for a focused conversation:

- **Goal** — What do you want to achieve from this conversation? (not the project goal — the conversation goal)
- **Reality** — What's happening now? What have you already tried? What's getting in the way?
- **Options** — What could you do? What else? What if constraints were removed?
- **Will / Way forward** — What will you actually do? By when? What support do you need?

Don't rush to Options. Most coaching conversations spend too little time on Reality — the person has a fuller picture than you do, and drawing it out surfaces information that changes what Options make sense.

## Powerful questions

Questions that open up thinking rather than narrowing it:

- "What would need to be true for this to work?"
- "What's the one thing, if resolved, that would unblock everything else?"
- "What are you most afraid of here?"
- "If you already knew the answer, what would it be?"
- "What would you do if you knew you couldn't fail?"
- "What's the impact on the team if this isn't resolved?"
- "Who else has a stake in this decision?"
- "What's the story you're telling yourself about why this is hard?"
- "What would you advise someone else to do in this situation?"

## Tuckman's model (team development)

Teams move through predictable stages — but not always linearly. A change in membership, leadership, or goal resets the clock:

| Stage | What it looks like | SM/Coach response |
|-------|--------------------|-------------------|
| **Forming** | Polite, uncertain, dependent on leader | Set clear norms; build safety; explain roles |
| **Storming** | Conflict, resistance, jockeying for position | Name what's happening; facilitate disagreement constructively; don't suppress conflict |
| **Norming** | Collaboration emerging; roles clarifying | Reinforce positive patterns; hand responsibility to the team |
| **Performing** | High autonomy; team solves its own problems | Get out of the way; focus on systemic impediments; coach the edges |
| **Adjourning** | Team disbanding | Mark the ending; celebrate; help individuals transition |

Most agile teams are caught in a **storming-norming loop** — they never fully reach performing because membership keeps changing or leadership intervenes too much.

## Psychological safety

Without it, teams cannot inspect and adapt honestly. Retrospectives become superficial. Impediments stay hidden. Innovation stops.

**Signs it's absent:**
- Retrospectives surface the same safe topics every sprint
- People agree in the room and disagree in the corridor
- No one challenges the most senior person
- Mistakes are minimised or hidden rather than shared

**What builds it (Amy Edmondson's research):**
- Leaders model vulnerability — share their own uncertainty and mistakes
- Curiosity over blame — "What happened?" not "Who did this?"
- Frame work as a learning problem, not a performance problem
- Actively invite dissent — "What's the strongest objection to this?"

**What destroys it:**
- Punishing bad news
- Sarcasm or dismissal in public forums
- Retrospective feedback being used against team members
- "Brilliant jerks" being tolerated

## 1:1s

The SM/coach's 1:1 is not a status meeting. It's the space where the relationship is built and where honest feedback travels.

**Principles:**
- The agenda belongs to them, not you — ask "What's most useful to talk about today?"
- Ask about the person, not just the work — "How are you doing with everything?"
- What's said in the 1:1 stays there unless the person says otherwise — protect confidentiality explicitly
- Follow up on previous conversations — shows you listened
- Don't fill silence — let them think

**Watch for:**
- Changed energy or tone compared to previous sessions
- Themes that recur across multiple 1:1s — this is signal, not noise
- What they're NOT saying (probe gently: "Is there anything you haven't mentioned that you wanted to?")

## Conflict

Conflict is information. Suppressing it doesn't resolve it — it drives it underground where it's more destructive.

**Thomas-Kilmann modes:**

| Mode | When useful |
|------|------------|
| **Collaborating** | When both parties' concerns are important; time allows |
| **Compromising** | Quick resolution needed; both give something |
| **Accommodating** | Issue matters more to them than to you; relationship preservation |
| **Competing** | Quick decisive action needed; you're right on a safety/ethical issue |
| **Avoiding** | Issue is trivial; timing is wrong; need to cool down first |

**SBI feedback model (Situation → Behaviour → Impact):**
- "In yesterday's planning session [Situation], when you cut across Sarah's point [Behaviour], I noticed she went quiet for the rest of the meeting and the team seemed less willing to offer ideas [Impact]."

Name observable behaviour, not character or intent. Keep it specific and close in time to the event.
